Modals of Deduction & Speculation

How sure are you — and how do you show it?

The case

Regional People Analytics team · confidential internal note

Attrition on the Client Solutions team has jumped from 6% to 19% in one quarter. Nobody has a confirmed cause. What you have is partial and circumstantial:

  • A new team lead started 10 weeks ago.
  • Exit interviews mention "unclear expectations" twice, but three other exits gave no reason at all.
  • A restructuring rumor has been circulating since August — HR has not confirmed or denied it.
  • Engagement scores for this team were already trending down before any of this.

1. Notice the pattern

Click the modal expression in each sentence. Don't worry about naming the rule yet — just notice how sure or unsure each speaker sounds.

Everyday
She must be in a meeting — her calendar's blocked all afternoon.
HR — present
The new team lead might be unaware of the complaint; nobody's escalated it yet.
HR — present
That can't be the whole reason for the drop — engagement was already falling before the reorg rumor started.
HR — past
The attrition spike could have been triggered by the leadership change, but we can't confirm it yet.
HR — past
Three people must have missed the town hall — they're asking questions it already answered.
Everyday
She can't have seen the survey results; she'd have mentioned them in the meeting.

Once you've clicked all six, ask yourself: which ones sound almost certain? Which ones sound like a guess?

2. Concept check

Look back at the six sentences before answering.

In "must be," "might be," and "can't be," what form comes right after the modal?

Compare these two sentences. What's different between them?

She must be in a meeting — her calendar's blocked all afternoon.
Three people must have missed the town hall — they're asking questions it already answered.

Which pattern is used for deduction about the past — "must have missed," "could have been," "can't have seen"?

Which two words, in these sentences, signal the speaker is almost certain — not just guessing?

3. The rule

You've already found this — here it is written out.

Almost certain — no Guessing Almost certain — yes
CertaintyAbout nowAbout the past
Almost sure — yesmust + base verbmust have + past participle
Fairly sureprobably is/doesprobably + past simple
Guessingmight / could + base verbmight / could have + past participle
Almost sure — nocan't + base verbcan't have + past participle

One structure, two time zones. The modal tells your listener how confident you are — the verb form after it tells them when.

5. Application — your read on the case

Write 5–8 sentences giving your own analysis of the case below. You don't have enough information to be certain about anything — that's the point.

The case, again

Regional People Analytics team · confidential internal note

Attrition on the Client Solutions team has jumped from 6% to 19% in one quarter. Nobody has a confirmed cause. What you have is partial and circumstantial:

  • A new team lead started 10 weeks ago.
  • Exit interviews mention "unclear expectations" twice, but three other exits gave no reason at all.
  • A restructuring rumor has been circulating since August — HR has not confirmed or denied it.
  • Engagement scores for this team were already trending down before any of this.
Worth knowing: in English, when someone works out what's really going on from small, indirect clues, we say they're reading between the lines — for example, "Reading between the lines, I don't think the reorg rumor is the real problem." You'll be doing exactly that in this task.

Use at least three different certainty levels from the scale above (for example: one "must have," one "might/could," one "can't be" or "can't have"). Mix present and past where it fits your reasoning.
