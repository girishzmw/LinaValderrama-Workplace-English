02 — READING COMPREHENSION · 10 MIN

Rebuilding the First Ninety Days

Read the passage, then answer the questions below.

Reading passage

For years, the regional HR team used to run onboarding as a single, one-day orientation. New hires used to sit through a long presentation on policies, sign some forms, and go straight to their desks. Managers used to assume new employees would "figure it out" as they went along.

That changed in 2019. After exit interviews showed that people who left within six months often mentioned feeling lost early on, the HR team redesigned the process. They extended onboarding to ninety days and built in regular mentor check-ins. During the first pilot, while the team was still testing the new schedule, one new hire admitted she almost quit in her second week — not because of the job, but because no one had explained how decisions got made. That comment changed everything.

The team rolled out the new model company-wide the following year. Within twelve months, early attrition dropped by almost a third.

1. How did onboarding look before the redesign?

A single one-day orientation — a long policy presentation, paperwork, then straight to the desk, with no follow-up.

2. What triggered the decision to change it?

Exit interviews showed people who left within six months often said they'd felt lost early on.

3. What happened during the pilot that "changed everything"?

While the team was still testing the new schedule, a new hire admitted she'd almost quit — not over the job, but because no one had explained how decisions got made.

4. What was the measurable result after the new model rolled out?

Early attrition dropped by almost a third within twelve months.

5. Grammar noticing: find two uses of "used to" — what do they have in common?

"used to run", "used to sit through", "used to assume" — all describe repeated practices that no longer happen today.

6. Grammar noticing: find the one past continuous sentence — what was happening when the new hire almost quit?

"While the team was still testing the new schedule" — that's the background action in progress.

05 — IDIOM SPOTLIGHT

One idiom, tied to today's theme

A new expression, worth adding to how you talk about change at work.

Idiom

Growing pains

Meaning

The difficulties or problems that come up when something — a company, a system, a person — is growing, developing, or changing, especially in the early stages.

Example

"The new onboarding process caused some growing pains in its first few months, but the team adjusted quickly."
