The Passive Voice

Formal policy and process language — how organizations describe what happens without always naming who does it.

1

Look at what you marked. In every second sentence, the thing that used to be the object ("three packages," "the policy," "my car," "the merger," "dinner," "the audit") has moved to the front and become the subject. And notice something else: in several of these, the sentence never tells you who did it — no neighbor, no board, no "someone." Hold onto that observation for the next stage.
2

Concept check

Answer each question based on what you noticed above. There's no penalty for a wrong guess — the feedback is the point.

1. In "Three packages were left at my door," do we know who left them?

Exactly. The sentence works perfectly well without naming who did it — because in this case, it isn't the important part.
Look again — the word "neighbor" only appears in the first sentence, not the second.

2. In "was revised" and "was broken," what verb form comes right after was/were?

Right. Was/were (or am/is/are, or will be) always pairs with the past participle — the same form you already use with "have."
Not quite. Compare "was revised" with "has revised" — same verb form, different helping verb.

3. Which version sounds more like something you'd read in an official HR document — "HR revised the policy" or "The policy was revised"?

That's the instinct organizations lean on. Policy language usually foregrounds the change itself, not the department behind it — it reads as more objective and less personal.
Try reading both out loud. "The policy was revised" is the one that sounds like it belongs in a handbook.

4. In "The merger will be announced next month," is the person doing the announcing named?

Correct. "By the board" could be added, but it isn't necessary — the announcement itself is the news.
"The board" appears in the first sentence only. The second sentence drops it entirely.

5. Compare "The board will announce the merger" and "The merger will be announced." What happened to "the merger"?

That's the mechanical heart of the passive: the thing that received the action moves to the front and becomes the grammatical subject.
Look at where "the merger" sits in each sentence — it's still there, just in a different position.

3

The rule

This confirms what you just worked out for yourself.

Form

be + past participle  ( + by + agent, optional )

"Be" changes with tense: is/am/are, was/were, will be, has/have been, is/are being, must/should/can be...

Why organizations use it

  • The person responsible is unknown ("My car was broken into last week.")
  • The person responsible is obvious or unimportant to the message ("The audit must be completed by Friday" — everyone knows compliance owns this.)
  • The writer wants to sound objective and formal — this is why policies, procedures, and official announcements lean heavily on the passive rather than "we changed this."
  • The focus belongs on the result, not the doer ("The merger will be announced next month.")

Add the agent only when it matters

"The decision was influenced by market pressure" — here, naming the cause is the whole point, so "by" earns its place. Most policy language leaves it out entirely.

It stacks with modals

must be completed · should be reviewed · will be announced · can be extended — the same be + past participle pattern, just with a modal in front.

Phrase of the session: "set in stone"

Meaning: fixed and unlikely to change.

In passive-friendly context: "The new remote-work policy isn't set in stone yet — it will be reviewed again in six months."

Consolidation quiz

1. "The manager sent the report yesterday." → correct passive form:

Correct.

2. "The company must complete the audit by Friday." → correct passive form:

Correct — the modal "must" is followed by "be," then the past participle.
Remember: modal + be + past participle, in that order, with nothing extra in between.

3. Which sentence is passive and grammatically correct?

Correct — "are" plus the past participle "given."
Two things to check: is "be" present, and is the verb in its past-participle form?

4. "Someone is reviewing your application right now." → correct passive form:

Correct — the continuous passive needs "is being" + past participle.
The original is in progress right now, so the passive needs the continuous form: is being + past participle.

5. Which sentence correctly keeps the agent, using "by"?

Correct.
The agent always needs "by" in front of it, and "be" needs to stay in the sentence.

6. Which sentence has the tone most appropriate for a formal policy document?

Correct — this is the register question, not just the grammar question.
Think about which one sounds least like a specific person is speaking.
